Quick answer

Already home and left something in Japan? You may still be able to get it back. First, contact the hotel, railway company, or other facility holding your item. Confirm the holding deadline and whether they can ship it to you overseas or only within Japan. If they can only ship domestically, ask whether they can release the item to someone you authorise in Japan. That person may be able to receive it and forward it overseas, depending on the item and destination. If they've already told you they can't ship overseas, skip to route 2 below — a receiver in Japan is the standard fix, and the check is free. If the item has been transferred to the police, follow the police claim process instead.

Most lost items in Japan are recoverable — if you move before the holding deadline. Here is the whole process:

  1. Contact the place where you lost it — the hotel, the railway company, or the airport. Not a general "Japan lost and found" office; there isn't one that covers everything.
  2. Ask four things: Is it being held? Until what date? Can they ship it directly to your home country? If not, can they ship it inside Japan?
  3. If they can ship it straight to you, let them. That is the fastest and cheapest outcome, and you do not need any service — including mine.
  4. If they will only ship inside Japan (this is the common answer), someone in Japan needs to receive it, settle any cash-on-delivery charge, and forward it to you.
  5. If it has already been handed over to the police, the process changes completely — see the police section below.

Already back home? When the facility won't ship overseas

This is the situation most readers of this page are actually in, so here it is in one place.

Being home does not end the matter. The facility keeps holding your item until its deadline, and the deadline — not your location — decides whether recovery is still possible. The only thing that changes once you're home is the shipping route. There are three:

1. The facility ships to you directly. Some hotels, especially larger ones, will do this if you can pay the postage. Always ask first: it is the fastest and cheapest route, and if they say yes, you need nothing else on this page.

2. The facility ships inside Japan only. This is the common answer, and it is a routing problem, not a refusal. The parcel needs a Japanese address: the facility sends it there (usually cash on delivery), and the person at that address settles the charge, checks the item, and forwards it to you with a proper customs declaration. That person can be a friend in Japan — or a service. What to do this week, in order:

  1. If you haven't emailed the facility yet, do it today, using the templates below — ask: held? until when? direct shipping, or domestic only? If they've already replied 'domestic only,' go straight to step 2.

The clock: how long you have

Deadlines in Japan are real, and they are the reason lost items become unrecoverable. Ask about the deadline in your very first message.

  • Hotels, hostels and ryokan usually keep lost property for 1 to 3 months. It varies a lot by property — some list a 3-month holding period, while smaller places may clear things out sooner. Food and perishables are thrown away quickly — JR Central, for example, may dispose of food with a short shelf life, and chilled or frozen food within 24 hours of finding it.
  • Trains and stations are much faster. A station usually holds an item for only a few days before moving it to a central lost-property office, and the railway hands unclaimed items to the police within about a week — JR West, for example, transfers items to the police within 7 days of the day they were found. JR East's holding period varies by station, and JR Central moves items to its Lost & Found Center in Aichi, where they can be claimed for up to 3 months from the day they were handed in. If you lost something on a train, treat it as urgent even if the item itself isn't valuable.
  • Police generally keep reported lost property for three months after receiving it. Some items, such as umbrellas and clothing, may be sold or disposed of sooner — as early as two weeks after the public notice. Confirm the exact claim deadline with the police. After that period, ownership can pass to the finder or the prefecture, and at that point you can no longer get it back.

So the order of questions matters: held? until when? comes before everything else.

Before you contact them, gather this

Facilities match items against their log using the details you give them. A vague message ("I lost a black bag") rarely gets a useful answer. Before you write, collect:

  • The name the booking was under and your stay or travel dates
  • A precise description: colour, brand, size, contents, anything distinctive — a scratch, a sticker, a charm
  • A photo of the item, if you have one from the trip (this is the single strongest proof it's yours)
  • Serial number for electronics, if you know it
  • Where you last saw it: room, train car, seat number, station, shop

You will also be asked for some of this again if the item is found, so keep it all in one place.

If you left it at a hotel, hostel or ryokan

This is the most common case, and the most recoverable.

The front desk or housekeeping will have logged the item, usually with the room number and the date it was found. Email the property directly — either the contact form on their website or the address on your booking confirmation works. Write in Japanese if you can (there is a copy-paste template below); a message in English often works at larger hotels, but smaller properties may reply slowly or not at all.

A typical reply, translated, sounds like this:

"We are keeping a jacket matching your description. We can send it within Japan by cash on delivery. We are unable to send parcels overseas."

That is actually good news. It means the item exists and there is a way to get it back to you. What the hotel is saying is that they can only send it within Japan — see the section below on why.

Three things to confirm with the hotel:

  1. The holding deadline — the exact last date.
  2. What they need to release it — commonly the booking name and dates; some properties ask for a copy of your ID, or a short written authorisation if someone else will receive it for you.
  3. How they will ship it domestically — most hotels send parcels cash on delivery (COD, called chakubarai), meaning the receiver in Japan pays the delivery charge on arrival. Typical domestic cost for a small parcel is roughly ¥1,200–2,300.

If the hotel says they can ship directly to your country, take that option. Some larger, foreigner-friendly hotels do offer it. Ask how to pay the postage — that is usually the sticking point, and the reason most properties refuse.

If you left it on a train or at a station

With trains, time matters most.

Each railway company runs its own lost-property desk — JR East, JR Central, JR West, and the private railways (Tokyu, Keio, Odakyu and so on) are separate. Shinkansen services are split between JR Central and JR West, depending on the line. You need the desk for the company you actually travelled with, and ideally the date, approximate time, line, and direction of travel. A phone left on a train may be logged at the terminal station, not the station where you got off.

Items found on trains move from the station to a central lost-property office, and then — within about a week — to the police. JR West, for example, hands unclaimed items to the police within 7 days of the day they were found; after that, the police hold them for 3 months. JR Central sends items to its Lost & Found Center in Aichi (no in-person pickup there — you can only get it back by domestic delivery), and items cannot be claimed after 3 months from the day they were handed in. JR East's holding period varies by station. So if you contact the railway a week later and they say the item is "no longer with us," ask the follow-up question: which police station or lost-property centre was it transferred to, and on what date? That answer is what lets you continue.

Release rules vary by operator. Some will ship domestically; some require collection in person or by an authorised person with documents. If the item is a phone or a wallet, the first 48 hours matter more than anything else on this page.

If you left it at an airport

Airports have two different desks, and travellers often contact the wrong one:

  • The terminal's lost-and-found handles items left in public areas — security checkpoints, toilets, seating areas, airport shops.
  • The airline handles items left on the plane or in the airline's lounge, under the airline's own rules and schedule.

If you lost it after security but before boarding, it could be with either desk. Contact the terminal desk first, and the airline as well if you may have left it on board a previous flight. As with stations, unclaimed items are eventually transferred to the police, so ask about the deadline and any transfer in your first message.

If you left it in a taxi

With taxis, one small habit makes all the difference: keep the receipt. A Japanese taxi receipt (ryoshusho) shows the company name and the car's number — with it, the company's lost-property desk can check the exact car you rode in.

Call the taxi company and give them the date and time, where you got in and where you got out, and what the item looks like. Drivers hand found items in at the end of a shift, so allow a day before concluding it isn't there. Unclaimed items are passed on to the police after a short holding period, so if the company doesn't have it after a few days, switch to the police process below.

No receipt? Harder, but not hopeless: if you paid by card or through a taxi app (GO, DiDi, Uber), the ride record identifies the company and the car. If you paid cash and kept nothing, try the taxi association in the city where you took the ride, and the police covering your route — but without a receipt or a ride record, the odds are slim.

If your item has gone to the police

Once an item moves from a facility to the police, the hotel process no longer applies. Different rules apply:

  • Under the Lost Property Act, facilities must hand found items to the police promptly, and railway operators typically transfer them within about a week (JR West: within 7 days of the day found).
  • Police generally keep reported lost property for three months after receiving it. Some items, such as umbrellas and clothing, may be sold or disposed of sooner: cheap everyday items can be sold if the owner is not found within 2 weeks of the public notice, so don't assume a low-value item will still be there at the 3-month mark. Confirm the exact claim deadline with the police.
  • To claim an item you need to prove it is yours: a detailed description, photos, serial numbers where relevant, and your ID. If someone else claims it for you, they will need a signed letter of authorisation (called an ininjo) and copies of your ID.
  • In practice, everything is handled in Japanese, and police stations keep standard business hours. If your identity can be verified, you can request return by mail at your own expense — but that mail is domestic, so the parcel still needs a Japanese address. If someone collects the item in person for you, they will need a letter of authorisation and ID documents, as above. Don't count on a police station shipping an item internationally.

Start by filing a Lost Property Report (ishitsubutsu todoke) and searching the police lost-property listings — most prefectural police publish a searchable database online, and reports can also be filed online in many prefectures. The National Police Agency's lost-property pages (in English) explain the system and link to each prefecture; for Tokyo, the Metropolitan Police lost-and-found pages (in English) cover both filing a report and searching what has been handed in. Keep the report reference number; police stations often will not confirm whether they have an item to a third party without it.

For police-held items, my service is guidance only — how to file the report and where your item is most likely being held. I don't charge a recovery fee for police cases, because release usually has to happen in person and a paid "recovery" would usually end in disappointment. Passports are outside the service entirely — they follow their own track, covered in the passport notes in the item section below.

If what you lost was a phone, wallet, IC card or passport

Where you lost it matters most, but what it is changes a few things too.

A phone

Speed matters more with a phone than with anything else on this page. Call it, and use Find My (Apple) or Find Hub (Google) before you contact anyone: a location result tells you which facility to call. When you do, give them the make, colour, case description and, if you have it, the IMEI or serial number — for a phone held by the police, the SIM or serial number is what they match it on. One shipping warning: a phone has a built-in lithium battery, and that restricts how it can be sent. Postal acceptance of battery-powered items varies by destination, so the carrier has to be checked case by case before anyone can give you a firm total (see the shipping section below).

A wallet

Wallets are handed in constantly in Japan. Items are logged carefully, and cash is usually still inside. If your wallet held bank or credit cards, freeze them anyway; it costs nothing and removes the one real risk. A wallet with ID inside is one of the easiest items to match, so name the ID type and any distinctive contents in your description. Wallets left on trains and in stations tend to reach the police quickly, so if the railway says the item is no longer with them, move straight to the police steps above.

An IC card (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA)

It depends on the card. A registered card may be reissued, with its balance protected — but only in person, at a station in Japan, with ID. You can't apply by phone or online: JR East, for example, handles reissue of a lost registered Suica at the station, under its Suica card rules. The cards that may qualify are the named ones: a My Suica or a Suica commuter pass; a registered PASMO or a PASMO commuter pass (see PASMO's official guide); and, in the JR West area, an ICOCA commuter pass or a child ICOCA (see JR West's ICOCA page). Conditions differ by issuer, so check with the company that issued your card. An unregistered card — the plain kind most visitors buy — generally cannot be reissued at all: the balance cannot be refunded or transferred to you remotely, and in practice it is gone unless someone in Japan can deal with it in person on your behalf. A found IC card is logged like any other lost property, so one inquiry to the railway is still worth it; just don't build your plans around getting the balance back.

A passport

A passport follows its own track, and it is the one item my service cannot help with at all. What you do depends on where you are.

Still in Japan: file a Lost Property Report with the police — found passports are handed to the police, and the report is also the paper trail your embassy may ask for — then contact your embassy or consulate about an emergency travel document. Embassies deal with lost passports every day and have their own procedures, so start there, not with a recovery service.

Already back home: you no longer need an emergency travel document. Report the loss to your own country's passport authority and follow its procedure for a replacement, keeping the Japanese police report reference if you have one. And if the passport turns up after a replacement has been issued, it goes back through official channels, not by private forwarding.

What the facility's reply usually means

A few patterns come up again and again:

  • "We can send it within Japan, cash on delivery." — The item is found and there is a workable route. You need a Japanese receiving address. This is the most common good outcome.
  • "Please send a copy of your ID / a letter of authorisation." — Normal. Hotels release items carefully because they are liable if they hand property to the wrong person. It is not a refusal.
  • "It has been handed to the police." — Ask which station or centre, and when. Then switch to the police process above, and move quickly — the police's 3-month clock runs from the day they received the item, not from the day you lost it.
  • "We have no record of it." — Ask once more with the photo and the exact dates, and check the nearby alternatives: a different branch, the taxi company, the station. If two attempts get the same answer, the item is most likely not there.
  • No reply at all. — Small properties sometimes only handle inquiries by phone, in Japanese, during business hours. Silence is not the same as "not found."

Why hotels in Japan often can't ship your item overseas

Travellers often assume a refusal is laziness. It usually isn't. There are practical reasons:

  • The customs declaration. To send a parcel overseas, the sender must declare what is inside and what it is worth — for someone else's belongings, in a bag they didn't pack. Most front desks won't sign their name to that.
  • Postage can't be collected on delivery. International mail has no COD. The hotel would have to pay Japan Post up front and then somehow charge a guest who has already left the country — they have no way to bill you.
  • Their whole routine is set up for domestic shipping only. Japanese hotels return lost items every week using domestic couriers, paid by the receiver on arrival. International shipping is outside that routine: different forms, different counter, different rules.

Understanding this matters because it tells you what the actual problem is. The item usually isn't stuck — the route is. What a recovery service really provides is the missing link: a Japanese address to receive the parcel, someone to settle the COD charge on arrival, a photo check so you can confirm the item is yours before paying international postage, and someone who completes the customs declaration and ships it to you.

If your hotel can ship directly to you, none of that is necessary. Take them up on it.

Questions travellers actually ask

Can I still get my item back after I've left Japan?

Yes, in most cases. Your location is not the obstacle — the holding deadline is. Contact the facility, confirm the item is there and how long they will keep it, then work out how to get it shipped: some facilities will post it to you directly, and if yours will only ship inside Japan, someone in Japan receives it and forwards it (see the back-home section above). If the police have it, the three-month clock — counted from the day the police received it — is the one that matters.

Will a Japanese hotel ship my item overseas?

Usually not — and it is structural, not rudeness. International shipping needs a signed customs declaration for someone else's belongings and the postage paid up front, and a front desk has no way to bill a guest who has checked out. Some larger, foreigner-friendly hotels do offer it, so always ask. If the answer is no, ask for domestic shipping instead; that is the routine hotels actually run every week.

I left my phone on a train in Japan. What do I do?

Call it and check Find My or Find Hub first, then contact the railway company's lost-property desk today — with the date, time, line, direction and, if you know it, the car and seat. A phone may be logged at the terminal station rather than where you got off. Expect the railway to hand unclaimed items to the police within about a week, so if they no longer have it, ask which police station it went to. And note the shipping catch: a phone's built-in battery rules out some postal services, so which carriers can take it has to be checked first.

I left my wallet at a hotel in Japan and already checked out. What should I do?

Email the hotel directly — the address on your booking confirmation works — with your booking name, stay dates and a precise description, including any ID or cards inside. Freeze the cards in the meantime. Ask for the holding deadline and whether they can ship internationally or only within Japan. Wallets are among the most commonly handed-in items in Japan, cash included, so the odds are better than you might fear.

How long will they keep my item?

It depends on who has it. Hotels, hostels and ryokan usually keep lost property for 1 to 3 months. Stations hold items for only a few days before they move to a central office, and railways hand unclaimed items to the police within about a week. The police generally keep items for three months after receiving them — with one trap: cheap everyday items like umbrellas and clothing may be sold or disposed of sooner, as early as two weeks after the public notice. The full breakdown is in 'The clock: how long you have' above.

International postage for a single item under 1 kg runs from about ¥1,600 (Air Packet to Europe) to about ¥5,300 (EMS to the US) at Japan Post rates — the table above has the details, and US-bound shipments may carry customs and duty-prepayment costs on top.
