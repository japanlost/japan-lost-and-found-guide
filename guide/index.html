Lost Something in Japan? How to Get It Back From a Hotel, Train or Airport

Rules and rates checked November 2026.

The short version

Most lost items in Japan are recoverable — if you move before the holding deadline. Here is the whole process:

  1. Contact the place where you lost it — the hotel, the railway company, or the airport. Not a general "Japan lost and found" office; there isn't one that covers everything.
  2. Ask four things: Is it being held? Until what date? Can they ship it directly to your home country? If not, can they ship it inside Japan?
  3. If they can ship it straight to you, let them. That is the fastest and cheapest outcome, and you do not need any service — including mine.
  4. If they will only ship inside Japan (this is the common answer), someone in Japan needs to receive it, settle any cash-on-delivery charge, and forward it to you.
  5. If it has already gone to the police, the process changes completely — see the police section below.

If the facility has already told you they can't ship internationally, or their reply is in Japanese and you can't tell what they're asking for, that's the point where I can step in. The clock: how long you have

Deadlines in Japan are real, and they are the reason lost items become unrecoverable. Ask about the deadline in your very first message.

  • Hotels, hostels and ryokan usually keep lost property for 1 to 3 months. It varies a lot by property — some publish 3 months, small places may clear things out sooner. Food and perishables are thrown away quickly — JR Central, for example, may dispose of short-shelf-life and chilled or frozen food within 24 hours of finding it.
  • Trains and stations are much faster. A station usually holds an item for only a few days before moving it to a central lost-property office, and the railway hands unclaimed items to the police within about a week — JR West, for example, transfers items to the police within 7 days of the day they were found. JR East's holding period varies by station, and JR Central moves items to its Lost & Found Center in Aichi, where they can be claimed for up to 3 months from the day they were handed in. If you lost something on a train, treat it as urgent even if the item itself isn't valuable.
  • Police hold lost property for 3 months from the public notice under Japan's Lost Property Act — the notice the police post after receiving the item, not from the day you lost it. After that period, ownership can pass to the finder or the prefecture, and recovery is effectively over.

So the order of questions matters: held? until when? comes before everything else.

Before you contact them, gather this

Facilities match items against their log using details. A vague message ("I lost a black bag") rarely gets a useful answer. Before you write, collect:

  • The name the booking was under and your stay or travel dates
  • A precise description: colour, brand, size, contents, anything distinctive — a scratch, a sticker, a charm
  • A photo of the item, if you have one from the trip (this is the single strongest proof it's yours)
  • Serial number for electronics, if you know it
  • Where you last saw it: room, train car, seat number, station, shop

You will also be asked for some of this again if the item is found, so keep it in one place.

If you left it at a hotel, hostel or ryokan

This is the most common case, and the most recoverable.

The front desk or housekeeping will have logged the item, usually with the room number and the date it was found. Email the property directly — the contact form on their website or the address on your booking confirmation both work. Write in Japanese if you can (there is a copy-paste template below); a message in English often works at larger hotels, but smaller properties may reply slowly or not at all.

A typical reply, translated, sounds like this:

"We are keeping a jacket matching your description. We can send it within Japan by cash on delivery. We are unable to send parcels overseas."

That is actually good news. It means the item exists and there is a route. What the hotel is saying is that their system ends at Japan's border — see the section below on why.

Three things to confirm with the hotel:

  1. The holding deadline — the exact last date.
  2. What they need to release it — commonly the booking name and dates; some properties ask for a copy of your ID, or a short written authorisation if someone else will receive it for you.
  3. How they will ship it domestically — most hotels send parcels cash on delivery (COD, called chakubarai), meaning the receiver in Japan pays the delivery charge on arrival. Typical domestic cost for a small parcel is roughly ¥1,200–2,300.

If the hotel says they can ship directly to your country, take that option. Some larger, foreigner-friendly hotels do offer it. Ask how to pay the postage — that is usually the part they can't solve, and the reason most properties refuse.

If you left it on a train or at a station

Trains are the time-critical case.

Each railway company runs its own lost-property desk — JR East, JR Central, JR West, and the private railways (Tokyu, Keio, Odakyu and so on) are separate. The Shinkansen are split between JR Central and JR West depending on the line. You need the desk for the company you actually rode, and ideally the date, approximate time, line, and direction of travel. A phone left on a train may be logged at the terminal station, not the station where you got off.

Items found on trains move from the station to a central lost-property office, and then — within about a week — to the police. JR West, for example, hands unclaimed items to the police within 7 days of the day they were found; after that, the police hold them for 3 months. JR Central sends items to its Lost & Found Center in Aichi (no in-person pickup there — collection is by domestic delivery), and items cannot be claimed after 3 months from the day they were handed in. JR East's holding period varies by station. So if you contact the railway a week later and they say the item is "no longer with us," ask the follow-up question: which police station or lost-property centre was it transferred to, and on what date? That answer is what lets you continue.

Release rules vary by operator. Some will ship domestically; some require collection in person or by an authorised person with documents. Be honest with yourself about timing: if the item is a phone or a wallet, the first 48 hours matter more than anything else on this page.

If you left it at an airport

Airports have two different desks, and travellers often contact the wrong one:

  • The terminal's lost-and-found handles items left in public areas — security checkpoints, toilets, seating areas, airport shops.
  • The airline handles items left on the plane or in the airline's lounge, under the airline's own rules and timelines.

If you lost it after security but before boarding, it could be either. Contact the terminal desk first, and the airline as well if you may have left it on board a previous flight. As with stations, unclaimed items are eventually transferred to the police, so ask about the deadline and any transfer in your first message.

If your item has gone to the police

Once an item moves from a facility to the police, forget the hotel-style process. Different rules apply:

  • Facilities hand found items to the police promptly — under the Lost Property Act, an ordinary facility must submit found items to the police promptly, and railway operators typically transfer them within about a week (JR West: within 7 days of the day found).
  • Police hold items for 3 months from the public notice. After that, the window closes. One exception: cheap everyday items such as umbrellas and clothing can be sold by the police if the owner is not found within 2 weeks of the public notice, so don't assume a low-value item will still be there at the 3-month mark.
  • To claim an item you need to prove it is yours: a detailed description, photos, serial numbers where relevant, and your ID. Claiming through another person requires a signed letter of authorisation (called an ininjo) and copies of your ID.
  • In practice, police desks work in Japanese, during Japanese business hours, and release is in person or by domestic mail. International shipping by a police desk is, realistically, not something to plan around.

Your first step is your own: file a Lost Property Report (ishitsubutsu todoke) and search the police lost-property listings — most prefectural police publish a searchable database online, and reports can also be filed online in many prefectures. Keep the report reference number; police desks often will not confirm holdings to a third party without it.

I don't charge a recovery fee for police cases, because release usually has to happen in person and a paid "recovery" would mostly end in disappointment. Passports are outside the service entirely: if you lost a passport, report it to your embassy or consulate and to the holding police station — embassies have their own channels for documents, and I can't collect one on your behalf.

What the facility's reply usually means

A few patterns come up again and again:

  • "We can send it within Japan, cash on delivery." — The item is found and there is a workable route. You need a Japanese receiving address. This is the most common good outcome.
  • "Please send a copy of your ID / a letter of authorisation." — Normal. Hotels release items carefully because they are liable if they hand property to the wrong person. It is not a refusal.
  • "It has been handed to the police." — Ask which station or centre, and when. Then switch to the police process above, and move quickly — the police's 3-month clock starts from their public notice, not from the day you lost the item.
  • "We have no record of it." — Ask once more with the photo and the exact dates, and check the neighbouring possibility: a different branch, the taxi company, the station. If two attempts get the same answer, the item is most likely not there.
  • No reply at all. — Small properties sometimes only handle inquiries by phone, in Japanese, during business hours. Silence is not the same as "not found."

Why hotels in Japan often can't ship your item overseas

Travellers often assume a refusal is laziness. It usually isn't. There are practical reasons:

  • The customs declaration. To send a parcel overseas, the sender must declare what is inside and what it is worth — for someone else's belongings, in a bag they didn't pack. Most front desks won't sign their name to that.
  • Postage can't be collected on delivery. International mail has no COD. The hotel would have to pay Japan Post up front and then somehow charge a guest who has already left the country — they have no way to bill you.
  • Their whole system is domestic. Japanese hotels return lost items every week using domestic couriers, paid by the receiver on arrival. International shipping is outside that routine: different forms, different counter, different rules.

Understanding this matters because it tells you what the actual problem is. The item usually isn't stuck — the route is. What a recovery service really provides is the missing middle: a Japanese address to receive the parcel, someone to settle the COD charge on arrival, a photo check so you can confirm the item is yours before paying international postage, and someone who fills in the customs declaration and ships it to you properly.

And to repeat the honest part: if your hotel can ship directly to you, none of that is necessary. Use their offer.

Two cautions: Japan Post rates are scheduled to change on 1 February 2027, and US-bound parcels currently require duty prepayment (DDP) through an approved filing app before the parcel can be sent — the postage is not the whole cost for the US, and any quote should itemise that. Items with built-in lithium batteries (phones, earbuds, power banks) are restricted: EMS generally refuses them, and other carriers decide case by case, so a battery item needs a carrier check before anyone quotes you a total.
